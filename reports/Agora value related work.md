# Enrollment-as-welfare sets the Agora model apart

The closest prior work is **Halpern, Kehne, Procaccia, Tucker-Foltz and Wüthrich (AAAI 2023)**. They study approval votes where each voter judges only a few candidates, and they prove a large gap between non-adaptive and adaptive elicitation. Next come the **communication–distortion papers of Mandal, Procaccia, Shah and Woodruff (NeurIPS 2019, EC 2020)**, which limit how much each voter sends and bound the resulting welfare loss, and **stochastic probing with XOS objectives (Gupta, Nagarajan, Singla, SODA 2017)**, which covers our exact objective class ("best option's count of probed yes answers") under independent priors. Each ingredient of our model has a precedent. The combination was not found in any of the four literatures searched: a hard per-member budget of yes/no questions about the member's own approvals, welfare that counts only members who actually said yes, and a comparison of silos, central and grassroots settings with attention and messages as separate costs. Most of our technical results are elementary or follow from known tools: Hoeffding's bound for sampling without replacement, standard explore-then-commit, one-good-arm lower bounds, and leader simulation. They should be presented as clean baselines, not as technical contributions. The novel claims are the model, the enrollment rule (no commitment without a yes), the three-architecture comparison, and one interpretation that looks genuinely new. Under independent priors the gain from adaptivity for this objective is at most logarithmic in m, so the unbounded m/b gain of sharing must come from correlated tastes. Several references in the notes were never opened and must be read before citing (list in the fourth section).

### Bandits, adaptivity and collaborative exploration

The mapping is clean: agents are players or clients, options are arms, one answer is one pull. Three things differ everywhere. Each member–option pair can be "pulled" only once and the answer is deterministic. Each member caps at b pulls. Reward is the number of yes answers on the final option, which sits between simple regret (only the final choice matters) and cumulative regret (every pull matters) ([bandits notes, Bubeck–Munos–Stoltz](https://arxiv.org/abs/0802.2655)).

### Agents acting for users

This literature supplies the story, not the theorems. **Field evidence shows ratification fails when it is optional.** In Habermolt, only 8 of 91 users who submitted an opinion through a hosted agent ever revised one ([Habermolt](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.24413)). ## Five of six results are elementary; the framing carries the novelty

A reviewer who knows these literatures will see most of our theorems as quick. Saying so up front, and putting the weight on the model, is the stronger position.

**Silos guarantee exactly b/m.** Elementary. Its shape matches per-voter-budget distortion bounds (Kempe's welfare loss proportional to candidates over bits per voter, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.08129)) and the "one round, no speedup" result in collaborative exploration ([Tao et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1904.03293)). Neither implies it, because objectives and sampling differ, but neither leaves room for a novelty claim. Present it as the baseline.

**The needle instance: no setting beats b/m, so the worst-case gain is 1.** Elementary. Only the needle member's own questions can find the needle. It has the flavor of standard one-good-arm lower bounds and directly parallels Halpern et al.'s single-candidate remark ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.15608)). The message-tier counterpart is supported by the No Strict Majority bound ([Filtser–Talmon](https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.02206)) and multiparty disjointness ([Braverman et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1305.4696)). The conclusion that "over all instances the gain comes from shared tastes" is a framing claim, and a good one.

**Hidden common favorite: sharing reaches the optimum with two questions per member, a gain of m/b.** The construction is elementary. The interpretation is the most defensible new claim. With known independent priors, our objective is monotone XOS of width m under a partition-matroid probing constraint, so the Gupta–Nagarajan–Singla theorem bounds the adaptive-vs-non-adaptive gap by O(log m) ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1608.00673)). A fixed pre-agreed plan from a shared prior is something silo agents could run without communicating. If that holds, then an m/b gap cannot arise from independent tastes and must come from correlation across members. This step is an inference from the notes, not a checked proof: verify that silos can indeed implement any non-adaptive plan in our model before stating it. Halpern et al. already show a non-adaptive vs adaptive gap for approval committees, so position ours as the welfare analog for one option with per-member budgets, explained by correlation.

**Popular option with approval share alpha: explore-then-commit approaches the optimum as n grows.** The technique is known. It combines Hoeffding without replacement ([Bardenet–Maillard, Prop. 1.2](https://arxiv.org/abs/1309.4029)), a union bound over options, and the uniform-exploration argument ([Even-Dar et al.](https://www.jmlr.org/papers/volume7/evendar06a/evendar06a.pdf)). For b = 1 it is Colton's design with m options ([PDF](https://www.gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/1963-colton.pdf)). One small observation is new and worth a remark. If b ≥ 2, then explorers keep a spare question and can still be asked about the winner, so the only loss is misidentification, and the problem becomes plain fixed-budget best-arm identification. The literature also predicts the obvious reviewer question: a fully sequential elimination rule should beat explore-then-commit ([Garivier et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1605.08988)).

**A grassroots Agora can simulate the central one.** Elementary (one agent plays coordinator, at some message overhead). Two points need care. First, Shapiro's formal notion of grassroots requires obliviousness: a sub-community must be able to run correctly on its own ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.11299)). A protocol with one designated coordinator over a fixed member set may need a remark on what happens when communities merge. Second, if the paper says "grassroots," then readers from Shapiro's line will expect this definition.

**What is genuinely new** is the model and what it makes measurable. The first element is enrollment as welfare: a member counts only after saying yes, so elicitation is a hard constraint on welfare, not just an input to estimation. This is the cleanest novelty claim; none of the elicitation, distortion or bandit papers read has it. The second is that the person asked is also the beneficiary, which separates us from crowdsourcing, where workers are fleeting and answer about external items. The third is two separate cost tiers, attention and messages: prior work counts pulls and rounds, or sums the two (Reddy et al.). The fourth is a controlled three-architecture comparison against the exact optimum. CalBench is the only other such comparison found, and its agents never query users. The fifth is the "value of an Agora" as a ratio between settings, explained by correlated tastes. The confirmation-only rule also answers a documented failure of deployed agent systems (Habermolt, Simocracy, and autonomous commitment in Electric Elves), which gives the motivation a citable empirical anchor.

Two steelman objections deserve constructive answers in the paper. "This is stochastic probing with an XOS objective": yes, for the central setting under priors, and that connection is what explains why correlation drives the gain. Our contribution is the worst-case, prior-free comparison across information architectures. "This is approval GASP with elicitation": yes, and GASP's multi-activity, minimum-size version is the natural next step for the position paper's model.

## Related work fits in five short paragraphs

**Paragraph 1, elicitation under query limits.** Halpern et al. 2023: per-voter limit plus adaptivity gap, but representation of a committee from population queries; ours ties welfare to who was asked. Halpern–Hossain–Tucker-Foltz: per-voter cap, but exact computation of ranked rules. Lu–Boutilier: an adaptive central elicitor, our "central," without per-voter budgets. Bentert–Skowron: random-subset ballots, our silo baseline. Imber et al.: "unknown" ballots, which our rule turns into non-enrollment. Zimet–Alouf-Heffetz–Talmon: attention-aware, budgeted queries, central and ordinal.

**Paragraph 2, communication and welfare.** Mandal et al. and Kempe: per-voter bits vs welfare ratio, same shape as b/m, but one-shot and with welfare on true utilities. Filtser–Talmon: center plus sites with message bounds; sites know full votes. Bhattacharyya–Dey: sampling finds a popular winner; our setting must also enroll. Fish et al.: queries to LLM proxies, while we price the human questions.

**Paragraph 3, bandits and adaptivity.** Best-arm identification (Audibert et al., Karnin et al.) and explore-then-commit (Colton, Garivier et al.): exploration templates, but with repeated noisy pulls and no per-person cap. Collaborative and federated best-arm identification (Hillel et al., Tao et al., Shi–Shen, Reddy et al.): agents sharing arms; they count rounds, not attention. Gupta–Nagarajan–Singla: under independent priors the gap is logarithmic, so our m/b gain comes from correlated tastes.

**Paragraph 4, agents acting for users.** Scerri et al. and Horvitz: when to ask the human, for one user, with autonomous commitment allowed. Habermolt and the Agent Village: personal agents in collective processes, with ratification rarely exercised; we build ratification into the model. CalBench: architectures compared against an oracle, but users are never asked. Huang et al.: proxies querying principals under cognitive cost, in a central auction.

**Paragraph 5, crowds, distributed agreement, grassroots, activity selection.** CrowdScreen and Karger–Oh–Shah: question counts as cost, but about external items with fleeting workers. Becchetti et al. and Braverman et al.: coordinator-free agreement and message lower bounds, with agents that know their own preferences. Shapiro: the formal meaning of grassroots. Darmann et al. (GASP): the full-information core that our model adds elicitation to.

## Conclusion

The paper's center of gravity should move from the theorems to the model. The quick results work best as clean, honest baselines, explicitly linked to the elicitation, distortion and bandit precedents they echo. The weight should rest on two claims no prior work makes. The first is that when nobody can be committed without saying yes, attention becomes a direct cap on welfare. The second is that the whole value of sharing comes from correlated tastes, which the stochastic-probing bound makes precise. The open threats to novelty sit in unread work closest to home: Poupko–Shapiro–Talmon's grassroots personal agents and the Kalech/Naamani-Dery elicitation line. Open both before writing the related-work section. Two natural extensions come straight from the literature: a sequential elimination rule to beat explore-then-commit, and a one-round grassroots protocol in the spirit of Hillel et al.
