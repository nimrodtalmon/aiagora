# Overload is documented; the peak is new

The literature strongly supports the *mechanism* behind the AI-Agora scale paradox, but not the *paradox itself*. Larger broadcast channels carry more traffic, active posting does not keep up, people mute and leave, and many hold back posts so as not to "spam" a large audience. Economic theory already treats receiver attention as a common-property resource that cheap broadcast over-uses and that crowds out weak or niche signals. Critical-mass sociology already argues that larger populations reach a threshold of k interested people more easily, *if* those people can find one another. What nobody has stated, formally or empirically, is the combination: threshold-k initiatives proposed through one channel whose per-post cost falls on all n readers, producing a number of formed groups that rises and then falls in n, against agent routing on noisy estimates that keeps it rising. Two empirical links are missing: no study measures whether *niche* proposals specifically fall with channel size, and no study shows an inverted U in *member count* (Twitch shows one in message rate). Some evidence cuts against the thesis. Size also attracts members and motivates contributors, and ranked feeds with strong moderation keep quality steady after growth. The paper's contribution is therefore the model and the size result, built on Anderson–de Palma and Falkinger for the channel, Oliver–Marwell for the size argument, and approval group activity selection for formation. Reading status is marked as **full**, **partial** (named sections read), **abstract**, or **metadata**. Nothing below goes beyond what was read.

## Channels lose per-member activity past a threshold, but niche suppression is unmeasured

The overload half of the thesis holds across email lists, Usenet, IRC and Twitch. In **206 email lists**, size predicts communication volume with an elasticity of **2.94**, and volume and variety independently predict member loss. Larger lists lose a larger share of members, yet size also predicts member *gain*. Butler's net verdict is that "size does not seem to have a net impact on the sustainability", while "the net impact of communication is negative" (**full**, via the 1999 dissertation chapter, not the 2001 article) ([Butler 1999](https://core.ac.uk/download/233992683.pdf); published as [Butler 2001, ISR](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/isre.12.4.346.9703), **abstract**). This is the best-measured fact for the model: what hurts is volume, not size as such, which is exactly the pollution mechanism. Across **2.65 million Usenet posts**, users facing overload answer simpler messages, write simpler replies, and are more likely to stop participating (**abstract**) ([Jones, Ravid & Rafaeli 2004](https://cris.bgu.ac.il/en/publications/information-overload-and-the-message-dynamics-of-online-interacti/)). IRC discourse stays at about **40 posters per 20 minutes** even with hundreds of users present (**abstract**) ([Jones et al. 2008](https://digitalcommons.njit.edu/fac_pubs/12495)). The one inverted U comes from **1.28 billion Twitch chat messages**. Messages per user rise with channel traffic, peak at about **40 messages per 5 minutes**, and then fall, with more copy-paste and less information per message. For most users the pattern also holds individually (**full**) ([Nematzadeh et al. 2016](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1610.06497v1.pdf)). Its x-axis is message rate, not member count, and the authors have no data on viewers who never chat.

Self-censorship is documented, though never for niche content specifically. In mailing lists, "spamming large audiences" was the most troubling reason not to post in the large lab list (**62%**) and in 28 other lists (**61%**), against **43%** in a small, high-traffic dorm list. Lab-list members underestimated its size by an order of magnitude (**full**) ([Zhang, Ackerman & Karger 2015](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~axz/papers/mailinglists.pdf)). Across **3.9 million Facebook users**, group posts were the most self-censored location (**38%**). The authors conclude that people censor more "when their audience is hard to define" and also "when the relevance or topicality of a CMC 'space' is narrower" (**full**) ([Das & Kramer 2013](https://research.facebook.com/file/849197482668435/self-censorship-on-facebook.pdf)). The second clause complicates the niche story: a broad channel deters posts that might spam people, while a narrow one deters off-topic posts. In experiments, broadcasting rather than narrowcasting moves sharing away from content useful to the recipient, but the manipulation is one person vs "a group", with no gradient of sizes (**partial**) ([Barasch & Berger 2014](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2014/05/Barasch-Berger-2014,-JMR_1.pdf)).

Neighborhood channels match the predicted *shape*, but only qualitatively. Nextdoor content in Atlanta is "functional rather than social". Users turned off email notifications "due to volume", called a temporary merger of neighborhoods "ridiculous", and said neighborhoods "sometimes got too large to post the things they wanted to say", so they moved to curated email lists and Facebook groups. That is the niche exodus the thesis predicts, from **13 interviews** (**full**) ([Masden et al. 2014](https://www.cc.gatech.edu/home/keith/pubs/nextdoor-chi2014.pdf)). In Dutch WhatsApp groups, muting is the main coping strategy. A crime-group moderator says muting is what breaks the group in an emergency (**full**) ([Mols & Pridmore 2020](https://repub.eur.nl/pub/135402/Mols-Pridmore-MM-C-Always-available-via-WhatsApp.pdf)). The more than **8,000** registered neighborhood crime-prevention groups survive by restricting themselves to alerts under the "SAAR" house rules, enforced by removal: an institutional answer to pollution (**full**, relevant sections) ([Mols & Pridmore 2019](https://ojs.library.queensu.ca/index.php/surveillance-and-society/article/download/8664/8724)). No quantitative study was found that links WhatsApp, Telegram, Slack, Discord or Nextdoor group size to posting, muting or leaving rates.

The evidence against deserves a place in the paper. When the Chinese Wikipedia block shrank the audience, contributions by *unaffected* editors fell **42%**, because contributors "care about the number of beneficiaries" (**full**, 2007 working paper) ([Zhang & Zhu 2007](https://archive.nyu.edu/bitstream/2451/28501/2/Zhang-Zhu_07-22.pdf); [AER 2011](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:aea:aecrev:v:101:y:2011:i:4:p:1601-15)). A larger audience thus also raises the motivation to propose. Ten subreddits added to Reddit's default set (one grew from about 240,000 to over 3 million subscribers) "remain high-quality", but activity clusters on fewer posts after growth (**full**, key results) ([Lin et al. 2017](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/ICWSM/article/download/14884/14734)). So ranking and moderation can protect average quality while minority posts lose visibility. A 33-year cross-platform preprint finds that re-engagement falls with community size, "with niche environments as a notable exception" (**partial**, not peer-reviewed) ([Nudo et al. 2025](https://export.arxiv.org/pdf/2501.12076)). Read together, these studies suggest an inverted U in *effective* size: too few beneficiaries at small n, too much volume at large n. This is a synthesis across studies, not a finding of any one of them.

## Attention-congestion economics already models the polluted channel, for one reader

The formal channel side is mature. Anderson and de Palma model receiver attention as "a common property resource". Senders ignore the crowding they impose, and a receiver who cannot tell messages apart examines them at random. When sending is cheap, "too many messages may be sent, or the wrong ones", and a small transmission *tax* raises total surplus. Their Proposition 5 allows multiple equilibria, including collapse, so the market can be "closed down when it ought optimally to be functioning" (**full**, 2008 working version) ([Anderson & de Palma 2009](https://economics.virginia.edu/sites/economics.as.virginia.edu/files/2025-04/inoRAND081110with%20figs.pdf)). Collapse matters for the Agora: the falling arm may be a tipping point, not a gentle slope. With multiple messages per sender, an equilibrium can consist of "a small range of loud shouters" (**abstract** and list of propositions) ([Anderson & de Palma 2013](https://economics.virginia.edu/sites/economics.as.virginia.edu/files/2025-04/shoutej101215_0.pdf)). Falkinger comes closest to niche crowding. In an attention-scarce economy, a larger reach "raise[s] the minimal signal strength required to be attended", and convergence of local to aggregate diversity "happens through a decline in aggregate diversity". Merging two attention-scarce economies adds no per-person diversity (**full**, Props. 1–5) ([Falkinger 2005](https://docs.iza.org/dp1538.pdf)). Proposition 5 is a formal counterpart of the merged-Nextdoor complaint above (our reading).

Every remedy in this line moves screening onto senders. Van Zandt shows that a sending tax "benefits all the senders" when their "information about the receivers is sufficiently accurate" (**abstract**) ([Van Zandt 2004](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:rje:randje:v:35:y:2004:3:p:542-560)). This is the closest existing statement of the agent claim, and it names the condition the Agora result must reproduce: routing beats broadcast when the estimates are good enough. Loder, Van Alstyne and Wash price first contact with an "attention bond" (**abstract** and framing) ([Loder et al. 2006](https://rickwash.com/papers/spam-bejeap.pdf)). One counterpoint is relevant: with limited attention, firms "may become incentivized to behave as mass advertisers, despite perfect targeting abilities" (**abstract**) ([Hefti & Liu 2020](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/randje/v51y2020i2p402-420.html)). Agents that can target will not necessarily do so if they are strategic. None of these papers has n readers on one channel with heterogeneous fit, a group threshold, or results in community size. Van Zandt's model, Falkinger's 2007 JET paper and Johnson (2013) on targeted advertising were not read.

## Critical-mass theory supplies the rising arm and names the missing mechanism

Oliver and Marwell give the size logic in words. If the cost of a collective good rises with the number who share it, larger groups act less. If it does not, larger groups act more, because they are "more likely to have a critical mass of highly interested and resourceful actors". They quote Marwell: "the larger the university, the greater the chance it has to get a minimum number" (**full**) ([Oliver & Marwell 1988](https://users.ssc.wisc.edu/~oliver/wp/wp-content/uploads/2014/08/5-paradox-of-group-size.pdf)). They then name the Agora problem: the few interested people in a large group struggle "to find one another", and the question is "whether there is some social mechanism that connects enough people" (same source). The scale paradox formalizes exactly this. The cost that grows with n is attention, and the connecting mechanism is the agent layer. Critical Mass III adds the "powerful effects of selectivity", an organizer concentrating effort on likely contributors under communication costs. This is the closest sociological ancestor of agent routing (**partial**) ([Marwell, Oliver & Prahl 1988](https://users.ssc.wisc.edu/~oliver/wp/wp-content/uploads/2015/06/MarwellOliverPrahlCritMassIIICORRECTED.pdf)).

Once members are known, the commitment step is already solved. Provision-point games with refunds fully implement the core (**partial**, theorems) ([Bagnoli & Lipman 1989](https://sites.bu.edu/blipman/files/2021/10/public_goods.pdf)), and Tabarrok's dominant assurance contract, which pays refunds if fewer than K accept, makes contributing dominant (**partial**) ([Tabarrok 1998](https://mason.gmu.edu/~atabarro/PrivateProvision.pdf)). The binding constraint in the Agora sits upstream: informing the right k people. Granovetter's threshold model shows that changing one person's threshold can turn a cascade of 100 into 1 (**full**) ([Granovetter 1978](https://www.gwern.net/doc/sociology/1978-granovetter.pdf)), so expect high variance near the peak in simulations. One correction for the paper: Esteban and Ray do *not* give an inverted U. They prove that larger groups are *more* effective whenever effort costs are at least quadratic in elasticity (**full**) ([Esteban & Ray 2001](https://debrajray.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/12/EstebanRayAPSR.pdf)). The Agora fits Oliver–Marwell's case of costs rising with size. No paper read derives non-monotonicity in size from communication costs.

## Routing systems and group activity selection each hold half of the agent story

The formation layer exists already. Approval group activity selection has preferences over (activity, size) pairs, lower bounds such as "a hike with at least 3 other people", and "maximum individually rational" assignments that maximize members served (**partial**, definitions) ([Darmann et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1401.8151); journal version [IJGT 2018](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00182-017-0596-4)). The social-network variant uses edges as "communication links", but only as a connectivity constraint, not a cost (**partial**) ([Igarashi et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1712.02712)). Event-based social networks take events as given and assign users centrally with full preference knowledge (**partial**) ([Liu et al. KDD 2012](http://hanj.cs.illinois.edu/pdf/kdd12_xliu.pdf); [Bikakis et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1801.09973)). Liu et al. also find that **84.61%** of offline Meetup friends live within 10 miles, which supports a local "community". The event-participant arrangement papers by She, Tong and coauthors, and Cheng et al. (ICDE 2017), are **metadata only**. Whether any of them has a minimum number of participants is unverified.

The routing layer also exists, without thresholds. Aardvark routes each question to a few likely answerers and caps how often each person is contacted, "spreading out the answering load across the user base" (**partial**) ([Horowitz & Kamvar 2010](https://www.ra.ethz.ch/cdstore/www2010/www/p431.pdf)). It needs one good answer, not k joiners, and it was never compared with broadcast. Recent LLM-agent work studies privacy leakage in agent social networks ([AgentSocialBench](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.01487), **partial**), pairwise matching ([Shang et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.11844), **abstract**), and matching under uncertain capability estimates ([CoWeaver](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2607.15545.md), **partial**). None compares a shared channel with agent routing for group formation. The one field deployment found shows that agent routing moves the bottleneck rather than removing it. At Edge Esmeralda, **505 intents** yielded **147 accepted introductions**, and the median time to both humans agreeing was **20 hours**, against **4.9 seconds** of agent negotiation ([Cosmos Institute](https://blog.cosmos-institute.org/p/we-gave-a-village-personal-ai-agents)). The agent arm therefore needs its own attention budget per member; otherwise its growth claim is not a fair comparison.

Each ingredient is known: threshold formation (GASP), broadcast congestion and the value of targeting (Anderson–de Palma, Falkinger, Van Zandt), and the rising arm in size (Oliver–Marwell). The new part has four pieces. The first is a channel read by all n members with heterogeneous fit, so one post's cost is paid n times while its benefit reaches a few. The second is threshold-k discovery that costs attention. The third is the number of formed groups as a function of n, proven to peak and fall under broadcast. The fourth is routing on noisy estimates that keeps it rising, with an explicit accuracy condition in the spirit of Van Zandt. Search coverage was limited, so read these before claiming novelty: Van Zandt 2004 in full, Falkinger 2007, Johnson 2013, the She/Cheng arrangement papers, hedonic games with thresholds, congested matching markets (Arnosti–Johari–Kanoria), Kairam–Wang–Leskovec 2012 on group life cycles, inverted-U results in platform economics, and, closest to home, Poupko–Shapiro–Talmon and the Kalech/Naamani-Dery elicitation line.

## Conclusion

The literature changes what the paper should claim. "Big channels get noisy" is settled and should be cited, not argued. The defensible new claim is structural: broadcast makes attention cost grow with n while the chance of reaching k interested people also grows, so the count of groups has an interior maximum. Anderson–de Palma's collapse equilibria suggest that the maximum can be a cliff. The strongest objection is not that the mechanism is wrong. It is that real channels fight it with house rules, side groups, ranking and motivation from a larger audience. The paper should treat these as alternative remedies to compare with, since SAAR rules and curated side lists are partial, manual forms of routing. On the empirical side, the most valuable contribution would be the missing measurement: niche proposal rates as a function of member count in neighborhood WhatsApp groups, which no source found has done.
