# Price the human yes, not the message

The best-motivated and least crowded direction for AI-Agora is **consent-gated group formation under a per-person question budget**. Many groups ("initiatives") each need a minimum number of members, a person joins only after saying yes to their own agent, and the goal is to serve as many people as possible with few questions each. The demand side is clear. In the one large field deployment, the **Edge Esmeralda Agent Village (239 agents, May 30 to June 27, 2026)**, agents settled negotiations in a **median of 4.9 seconds, while both humans took a median of 20 hours to say yes**. Popular people were swamped with requests, and fund allocations were recorded without owners confirming them. So human attention and ratification are the bottlenecks, not agent compute. The theory side is open. Two-sided "interview" matching became an active line in 2025 and 2026, but it stays bipartite and pairwise. No paper found treats non-bipartite groups with a minimum size, one-person questions and commitment only after a yes. For an app, the durable growth loop in group coordination is "the link is useless until others open it" (Doodle, Partiful, Splitwise). The big players have taken the "one shared AI in the group chat" slot (ChatGPT, Meta AI, Instinct). The open slot is forming groups among people who don't yet know each other, which today grows only through paid ads and city-by-city launches. A **"we need 2 more" threshold card** turns that stranger-matching problem into an invite loop, and it is exactly the theory problem made visible. The combined direction: write the threshold-group paper and run the card as its live testbed in a bounded community (a conference or campus cohort). The event log then supplies the paper's costs directly.

## Agents negotiate in 4.9 seconds; humans take 20 hours

Real many-agent, many-person deployments are still almost only research and festival pilots. Commercial products are either one shared bot in a group chat or pairwise agent negotiation (two calendars, or two people to introduce).

The failures repeat across these sources, and each one matches an element the Agora model already has. **Ratification is skipped when it is optional.** Simocracy recorded allocations before owners confirmed, and the Cosmos authors flag this as a gap for consequential decisions. In Habermolt, **82% of opinions were never revised**, and about half of all critiques followed a nudge email ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.24413)). **Attention overload falls on the receiving side.** Agents produced more possible connections than people could absorb, and after one talk four or five people messaged the same attendee ([Cosmos](https://blog.cosmos-institute.org/p/we-gave-a-village-personal-ai-agents)). **Agents misrepresent their people.** Village agents invented personal details and attributed made-up ideas to their owners. A WIRED reporter's dating twin hallucinated and used clichés ([WIRED mirror](https://wirednews.pages.dev/story/ai-agents-are-coming-for-your-dating-life-next)). **Agent voices drift toward the average.** Habermolt's autonomous opinions were more alike than interview-based ones (mean cosine similarity 0.745 vs 0.649), and 36 of 54 opinions in one deliberation began with the same phrase ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.24413)). **Agents grab the first offer**, as Magentic shows. Finally, the village agents used up a shared credit supply (a commons problem), and the harnesses were "not built for multiplayer use" ([Cosmos](https://blog.cosmos-institute.org/p/we-gave-a-village-personal-ai-agents)).

The practitioners' own summary is that **"individual alignment did not automatically produce collective alignment."** The Cosmos team proposes comparing human-led, agent-led-with-ratification and mixed designs, and layered, central and purely personal architectures, but has not tested them ([Cosmos](https://blog.cosmos-institute.org/p/we-gave-a-village-personal-ai-agents)). That proposed comparison is AI-Agora's silos / central / grassroots comparison. What is missing everywhere is outcome data: no deployment reports retention, wellbeing or controlled comparisons, and the 147 Edge intros are tracked only to "started chatting."

## Coordination links spread for free; stranger matching buys its growth

Group-coordination apps that last spread through one loop. The organizer creates something that is useless until the others respond, and the others can respond without an account. **Doodle reached about 10M monthly users by Oct 2011, and 98% of users came through a personal invitation or recommendation** ([TNW](https://thenextweb.com/news/its-not-sexy-but-it-sure-is-popular-group-scheduling-tool-doodle-hits-10m-monthly-users)). When2meet needs no login and no submit button ([TidBITS](https://tidbits.com/2021/01/06/when2meet-an-easier-way-to-settle-on-a-meeting-time/)). Splitwise grew to "tens of millions of registered users" organically, with no paid spend, and keeps its free tier useful enough that users invite friends ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2021/04/28/splitwise-raises-20m-series-a-to-help-everyone-in-the-world-divvy-expenses)). Partiful started as a text-message service so guests on any phone could join, and reached **about 500K monthly active app users in Q1 2025, up 400% year over year** ([CNBC via NBC New York](https://www.nbcnewyork.com/news/business/money-report/meet-partiful-the-gen-z-party-planning-staple-thats-taking-on-apple/6231135/)). Charging the guest side can break the network. Meetup's 2019 test of a per-RSVP fee would have raised a weekly 30-person group's cost by 1,210% and drew a backlash ([NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/tech/tech-news/meetup-was-darling-tech-industry-can-it-survive-wework-n1106676)).

Hype loops grow fastest and collapse fastest when the core use does not repeat. BeReal fell from about 15M to under 6M daily users between Oct 2022 and Mar 2023 ([Platformer](https://platformer.substack.com/p/how-bereal-missed-its-moment)). Clubhouse's cohort retention reportedly fell near zero by months 6–7, though that figure rests on one Medium analysis ([ProdLab](https://prodlab.substack.com/p/from-hype-to-hibernate-the-clubhouse)). Wordle survived because a daily three-minute habit stayed after the hype, and its shareable emoji grid took it from 90 players to over 2M in about two months ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wordle)).

Stranger-matching services follow a third pattern, and it is the weak one. **Timeleft grew through paid ads, opening about one new city per day in 2024, then pulled back from 325 cities to about 200–220** because small cities bored members with "the same ~10 people." Its target is 50+ weekly participants per city ([How They Build](https://timfrin.substack.com/p/inside-timelefts-journey-to-connecting)). 222 launches city by city, users don't choose whom they meet, and some users found its events awkward or forced ([Fast Company](https://fastcompany.com/91356813/222-aims-to-end-loneliness-by-engineering-chance); [Boston Globe/NYT, Oct 26, 2026](https://www.bostonglobe.com/2026/10/26/business/paying-friends-apps/)). These services have no built-in invite loop, because a member does not know whom to invite. The demand is still there: 47% of young adults say they want more friends to do activities with ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/09/18/bumble-bffs-revamped-app-is-here-focusing-on-friend-groups-and-community-building)). Incumbents are moving toward groups (Bumble BFF's Groups tab in Sept 2025, Tinder's Group Hangout in Sept 2026) without per-person agents ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/09/18/bumble-bffs-revamped-app-is-here-focusing-on-friend-groups-and-community-building); [Boston Globe/NYT](https://www.bostonglobe.com/2026/10/26/business/paying-friends-apps/)).

**The open slot** follows from this map. One shared assistant inside a known group is taken, and Instinct already adds personal agents that ask permission there. Nobody, in the products read, uses many personal agents with private knowledge of their people to form *new* groups among people who don't yet know each other, under a minimum size, with each person's yes. A group that is short of its minimum is a natural thing to share ("we need 2 more for the Sunday chess club"), so it gives stranger matching the invite loop it lacks. Public reaction to agents that "simulate" people is hostile: a digital-twin dating test was called "horrifying" ([Newsweek](https://www.newsweek.com/new-kind-of-dating-app-is-coming-some-say-horrifying-11828969)). So "agents ask you, they don't act as you" is a selling point, not only a safeguard.

## Five research problems, ranked by fit and open room

The ranking weighs three things: fit to the evidence, room for non-trivial theory, and distance from crowded lines. The table gives the model sketches. The paragraphs after it give the motivation, the closest work and what would be new. In every problem, "members served" means people placed in a group that actually forms, and it counts only people who said yes.

| Rank | Problem | Who | What is unknown | What agents may do | Objective |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Threshold groups with consent | n members, each with an agent; m initiatives, each with a minimum size (and possibly a cap) | Which initiatives each member would accept | Ask their own member at most b yes/no questions ("join initiative j if it forms?"); message other agents; place a member only after a yes; one group per member | Members served, compared with the full-information optimum, plus questions per person and questions wasted on groups that fail |
| 2 | Twin plus person | Same members; each agent holds a prediction ("twin") of its member with unknown error | True acceptances; how wrong the twin is | Use the twin for free; ask the real person up to k questions, chosen with the twin's help | Welfare when the twin is right (consistency) and when it is arbitrary (robustness), as a function of k; rate of rejected invitations |
| 3 | Grassroots vs central separations | The problem 1 instance under three architectures: silos, a central coordinator, peer-to-peer agents | As in problem 1 | Silos see only their member; the center sees all answers; peers exchange messages | Separations or equivalences on four measures: questions per person, messages and rounds per agent, privacy (what other agents learn), robustness to agents that drop out |
| 4 | Incentives of question order | Members report to their own agents; agents report each member's predicted flexibility to the protocol | Members' true types; agents' honesty | Members may misdescribe themselves; agents may shade flexibility to be asked first or to steer groups | A question-efficient protocol in which neither side gains from misreporting, approximately in large markets |
| 5 | Value of pooling correlated tastes | Members whose tastes share a hidden structure (types) | The types and each member's place in them | One member's answer updates other members' twins | Questions per person saved by pooling, against what pooling reveals about members |

**Problem 1 is the most Agora-specific and the least crowded.** It is the position paper's model (initiatives, acceptance sets, minimum size, single membership). Both deployment bottlenecks enter it directly. The human yes is the only way to enroll, and the per-person budget covers questions from *all* initiatives, so popular people are congested, as at Edge. The closest work is two-sided interview matching: Ashlagi, Chen, Roghani and Saberi need O(log² n) interviews per agent for an interim-stable matching ([ITCS 2025](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/storage/00lipics/lipics-vol325-itcs2025/LIPIcs.ITCS.2025.12/LIPIcs.ITCS.2025.12.pdf)). Babaioff, Gil and Romm get about 2 interviews per applicant in ex-ante equivalent markets ([arXiv 2602.20358](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.20358v1)). Rastegari et al. show that computing a minimum certificate is NP-hard and list decentralized policies as open ([EC 2013](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/~condon/papers/interviews.pdf)). On the full-information side, approval group activity selection with "maximum individually rational" assignments is exactly the problem without elicitation. It is hard in general and efficient under increasing preferences, which is what a minimum size gives ([Darmann et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1401.8151)). Three things would be new. Groups are non-bipartite and have a threshold, so questions are wasted when a group fails, and that breaks the clean deferred-acceptance structure. A question goes to one person, not to a pair. "Placed only after a yes" mirrors interim stability's "matched pairs must have interviewed" but turns it into the welfare count. Natural targets are an adaptivity gap (a fixed question list vs adaptive questions), O(log n) questions per person in random markets, and a worst-case lower bound. The single-option results in the earlier Agora draft are elementary baselines, and the earlier related-work review flagged this multi-initiative, minimum-size version as the natural next step.

**Problem 2 is the safest and the quickest to publish, but it sits closest to existing work.** Twins are now good enough to make "predict, then ask when unsure" realistic. Agents built from two-hour interviews reach 83–86% of a person's own two-week test-retest consistency, against 74% from demographics alone ([Park et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.10109)). Filos-Ratsikas, Kalantzis and Voudouris name the hybrid "partly predicted, partly elicited" setting as open. They also prove that fixed top-k elicitation of a constant number of values cannot give sublinear consistency with bounded robustness ([arXiv 2502.06489](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.06489v1)), which leaves adaptive, prediction-guided questions open. Boehmer, Fish and Procaccia treat LLM queries as noisy oracles with error-oblivious guarantees, but they never charge for asking the real person ([ICML 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.22939v1)). Amanatidis et al. show that two queries per agent give the best worst-case distortion for matching ([NeurIPS 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.01872v3)). Two Agora twists would be new. First, a twin cannot enroll anyone, so a wrong twin costs a rejected invitation, not a wrong commitment, and the guarantee bounds wasted attention. Second, Habermolt's homogeneity points to a better error model: twins are biased toward the average member, not noisy at random. This problem works as the single-decision warm-up inside the problem 1 paper.

**Problem 3 turns the earlier single-option architecture comparison into a full program.** The Cosmos team proposed this comparison but did not run it. Maheswaran et al. showed that "decentralization does not automatically guarantee superior protection of privacy" for scheduling agents ([AAMAS 2005](https://cs.huji.ac.il/course/2005/aisemin/articles2006/docs/pa8c3_1030.pdf)). CalBench compares channel structures against an exact oracle, but its agents never ask their users anything ([arXiv 2605.09823](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.09823)). The anchors for the measures exist. Distributed almost-stable matching takes polylog rounds with short messages ([Ostrovsky–Rosenbaum](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1408.2782)). Exact stability needs about n² queries in the worst case ([Gonczarowski et al.](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1405.7709)). Joint differential privacy formalizes "each person learns only their own assignment" ([Hsu et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1311.2828)). Ashlagi, Braverman, Kanoria and Shi already separate learning one's own preference from communication, the same two cost tiers as AI-Agora ([Management Science 2020](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10200022)). A clean target: in random markets, local protocols match the central one on attention up to polylog factors, the central one pays in privacy, and local ones pay in rounds. In worst-case markets, local protocols need polynomially more questions.

**Problem 4 has the strongest pull from failures but is the hardest.** Magentic showed models fully compromised by manipulation. Habermolt warns that misrepresenting agents could "quietly distort collective outcomes" ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.24413)). Dube proves that a person's gain from lying to their own proxy equals the proxy's "within-range regret," and lists matching and bargaining as open ([arXiv 2607.14357](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2607.14357.md)). Interview algorithms are not strategy-proof (Ashlagi et al.) and reach only approximate (ε-) Nash equilibria in random markets ([Cole–Jangir](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2506.19345.md)). What is new is the incentive in the question *order*. A protocol that asks the most flexible people first rewards agents that exaggerate flexibility, and nobody has priced that. Exact strategy-proofness is unlikely, so the realistic results are large-market approximations.

**Problem 5 is the formal "value of the Agora," and it works best as the outlook.** The earlier review found that, under independent priors, adaptivity gains for this objective are at most logarithmic (via stochastic probing with XOS objectives, [Gupta–Nagarajan–Singla](https://arxiv.org/abs/1608.00673)), so large gains from sharing must come from correlated tastes. Park et al.'s finding that combining sources about one person gave only "modest" gains points the same way: the savings come from across people. The problem is open-ended, and the privacy side links it to problem 3.

## Two app concepts, one of which is the theory

**Concept A, the threshold card ("we need 2 more").** A person tells their agent an intention, such as "a Sunday 8 a.m. run, 5 people, within 2 km." Other agents check it against what they know of their own people. Each plausible candidate gets one question: "join if it forms?" The group forms only when the minimum is reached, and no one is committed before that. While it is short, the group is a shareable card, and anyone can answer it from a web link without an agent. *Growth loop:* each card that is short of its minimum asks its members to recruit from their own contacts, which gives stranger matching a Doodle-style loop. Each formed group is proof that it works, and members start their own cards. *Theory inside the app:* this is problem 1 directly. Which candidates get asked, in what order, under a per-person question cap, decides whether the group forms and how many questions are wasted. Twins (problem 2) choose whom to ask. *Risks:* density and cold start (Timeleft's lesson), so launch inside bounded communities such as a conference, a campus or a course, where Edge and Ditto worked. Others are spam (a hard per-person weekly question cap is both the fix and the theory constraint), safety with strangers (verified communities first), and misrepresentation (agents only ask; they never speak for a person to strangers).

**Concept B, the "make this happen" link for a known group.** An organizer writes "dinner with these 6 next week" and sends a link. Each invitee's agent, or a three-question web form for people without one, answers privately on times, budget, diet and area. Agents propose two plans, and everyone taps yes. *Growth loop:* the proven Doodle/Partiful link, with each plan exposing N people who need no account. *Theory inside the app:* a small version of problems 2 and 3, multi-dimensional choice with few questions and privacy (show the plan, never who blocked what). *Risks:* this is the most crowded slot. ChatGPT, Meta AI and Instinct ship shared agents in group chats, and Instinct already has permission-asking personal agents. So B is useful as a familiar entry point (and to bring people onto the platform), but it cannot set the product apart.

Concept A is the one that could plausibly spread virally. It is the only one that both uses an open slot and has a loop built into the object itself.

## One direction: threshold groups, proven and deployed together

The combined direction is a paper on **consent-gated threshold group formation** (problem 1, with problem 2's twins as the agents' predictions), developed together with the threshold card as its live testbed. The model and the app share costs exactly. Questions per person is the attention tier. Agent-to-agent traffic is the message tier. Rejected invitations are wasted attention. Members served is welfare. The app's event log records every question, answer and formation, so the paper's measures come straight from deployment data, and each logged instance can be solved exactly offline (the full-information problem is approval group activity selection) to compare protocols against the optimum. The protocols to test are the theory's own: a fixed question list vs adaptive questions, twin-guided ordering, and central vs peer-to-peer coordination (problem 3). Simulated, LLM and human members sit behind the same interface. A pilot in one bounded community with a few hundred people (the scale at which Edge produced 147 accepted intros) would give the first controlled numbers in this space, which no deployment has published. It would also measure how many questions people tolerate, a figure no source provides.

## Conclusion

The evidence moves the Agora pitch from "agents save time" to "agents make consent cheap enough to scale." Agents already negotiate almost for free. What fails in practice is the human end: confirmations get skipped, popular people get swamped, and agents speak in a generic voice. A model that counts a person only after a yes, and caps the questions each person receives, is therefore the right abstraction, not a simplification. The same constraint that makes the theory non-trivial (the minimum group size, which wastes questions on groups that fail) is what makes the app shareable (a group that is short of its minimum has a reason to recruit).

The main strategic risk is being outrun on the known-group use case by OpenAI, Meta and Instinct. The main scientific risk is an unread paper on coalition formation with elicitation. Both point to the same move: commit to forming groups among strangers under a minimum size with consent, check the two novelty threats first, and treat the first bounded-community pilot as a data source for the paper, not just a product launch.
